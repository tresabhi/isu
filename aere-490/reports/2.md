Back to the topic of gravity from the last report. I spent some time developing equations describing the relationship between acceleration readings and voxels forming bodies. I have settled on a tensor equation of the form:

$$
\R^{d \times N} = \R \cdot \R^{d \times N \times Nd} \cdot \R^{Nd}
$$

This matrix-scalar-tensor-matrix of real numbers represents the relation between the acceleration (soon to be substituted with the Doppler effect), positions, and masses of voxels. The idea is that I split up the asteroid/body into many voxels (N\*d), as a function of the number of samples (N), where d = 3 is the dimension of our universe. This results in the following equations, the latter being expressed in the Einstein notation.

$$
A = G R M
$$

$$
a_{in} = G \frac{r_{inj}}{r_{nj}^3} m_j
$$

From here, it is easy to see how expensive solving for the M column vector will be. Take these bodies I developed for benchmarking, for instance:

![](https://i.imgur.com/XiqhPxz.png)

![](https://i.imgur.com/nVPp1Jc.png)

These are benchmark bodies I designed with 100,000 samples. I developed the following equation to scale the number of samples of accelerations to find a new suitable N to fit a cube:

$$
N' = \frac{1}{d} \left\lfloor (Nd)^\frac{1}{d} \right\rfloor^d
$$

This sizes our sample size to 95,832. That's a 66x66x66 grid, and it's already frying my RTX 3070 while generating the benchmark, let alone calculating the masses M.

Direct methods of solving for M are O(n^3). Numerical methods are still O(n^2). My RTX 3070, solving on CUDA cores powered by PyTorch, maxes out at about N = 300, which allows only for a 9x9x9 grid, making it useless for real-life applications.
